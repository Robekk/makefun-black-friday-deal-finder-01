Mirage watch

Discounts measured against a “comparable value” or a list price the product may never have sold at. The deals can still be good — but judge the dollar price, never the percentage.

How a mirage works

Some stores print a comparable value next to the sale price: a reference figure for a similar product, not a record of this one selling at that price. A “52% off” banner built on it is arithmetic, not evidence. Our rule on this page: a percentage only counts when the “was” price is a price the product actually sells at — its list price or its usual street price, as the named source showed it.

How we check

Every deal on this page passed the same four steps this morning.

  1. Find. Our agent swept the retailers’ own Black Friday releases and the established deals desks before dawn on Black Friday.
  2. Read the price where it lives. Each price was read at the store or in a deals source that names the store — never from a screenshot, a forum post, or another aggregator’s summary of a summary. The source is named on every card.
  3. Grade it honestly. The verdict compares the sale price with what the product usually sells for and what it has cost before: a record low, a real deal, a fair cut, a thin cut — or a mirage, when the percentage is built on a reference value.
  4. Stamp the time. Black Friday prices move by the hour, so every card carries the window in which its price was checked: . If a price has moved since, the store link shows today’s number.

What we left out

Anything we could not verify: deals whose “was” price no source would show, gift-card bundles dressed up as price cuts, and prices reported by only one unverifiable post. A shorter list of real deals beats a long list of maybes.
